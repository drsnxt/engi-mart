# Narrative and slide writing

The story is the deck. Design can amplify a clear argument; it cannot rescue a muddled one.

## Start with the audience

Before writing a title, answer:

- **Who** is in the room? What is their role, knowledge, and stake?
- **What do they believe now?** What will they resist?
- **What must they decide or do?** By when?
- **What will they remember** if they forget everything else?
- **What tone** fits the room: executive, technical, academic, persuasive, celebratory?

Write the answers down. If you cannot, you are not ready to design.

## The throughline

One sentence:

> By the end, **[audience]** should **[think / feel / do]** because **[evidence]**.

Examples:

- Weak: "An overview of our platform."
- Strong: "The pilot cut component-sourcing time by 40%, so the college should fund a full rollout this semester."
- Weak: "Research on battery materials."
- Strong: "Coating the cathode with a thin oxide layer extends cycle life by 30% at the same cost."

A throughline is a claim, not a topic. If no one could disagree with it, it is not saying anything.

## Argument shapes

### Answer first (Pyramid Principle)

Lead with the conclusion, then support it with a small number of grouped arguments, then evidence.

```
Answer
├── Argument 1 → evidence
├── Argument 2 → evidence
└── Argument 3 → evidence
```

Use for executives, investors, boards, and anyone who may read only the first slide. The title sequence is the argument.

**MECE test:** arguments should be mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. If two arguments overlap, merge them. If something important is missing, add it.

### SCQA

- **Situation** — shared context the audience accepts.
- **Complication** — what changed, what hurts, what is at stake.
- **Question** — the decision the complication forces.
- **Answer** — your recommendation.

Use SCQA to open a business review, a proposal, or a persuasive talk. It creates tension before the answer.

Example:

> **S:** Engineering students buy components from several local shops.
> **C:** Prices and stock are fragmented, so they waste trips and overpay.
> **Q:** How can a student find the best combination quickly?
> **A:** A basket-level comparison platform that ranks shops by total cost, distance, and availability.

### Problem–solution–proof

- Problem: who suffers, how much, and why now.
- Solution: what changes, and why it works.
- Proof: evidence, demo, traction, testimonials.
- Ask: the next step.

Use for product pitches, sales decks, and project demos.

### What is / what could be (Duarte)

Alternate between current reality and the better future:

```
What is → What could be → What is → What could be → New bliss → Call to action
```

Use for keynotes, change proposals, and anything that needs to move people, not just inform them. The contrast creates tension; the "new bliss" makes the future concrete; the call to action tells the audience how to get there.

### Three-act structure

- **Setup** — context, characters, stakes.
- **Confrontation** — the problem, evidence, failed alternatives, tension.
- **Resolution** — the idea, proof, new bliss, ask.

Use for talks, not for dense business reviews. Keep the acts visible with section dividers or verbal signposts.

### Storytelling devices

Use these sparingly; one or two per talk is enough.

- **Concrete detail** — one user, one shop, one receipt, one afternoon. Specifics are memorable; abstractions are not.
- **Tension** — state what is at stake and what happens if nothing changes.
- **Contrast** — before/after, then/now, what is/what could be.
- **The rule of three** — three examples, three reasons, three acts. The brain likes triads.
- **Open loop** — pose a question early and answer it later. Do not leave it open at the end.
- **Human proof** — a quote, a customer, a face, a story. Evidence makes the argument credible; a person makes it memorable.
- **The new bliss** — paint the after state in concrete terms before the call to action.
- **A specific ask** — a person, an amount, a date, a next step. Vague endings produce vague outcomes.

## The ghost deck

The highest-leverage step in presentation making. Write one action title per slide, in order, before designing anything.

Rules:

- A title is a complete sentence that states the conclusion.
- One title = one idea.
- If the title needs "and" or "but", split the slide.
- If the title is a label, rewrite it as a claim.
- If the title needs a paragraph to explain, the idea is not clear enough.
- Read the titles aloud. They should form a coherent argument.

Weak ghost deck:

1. Introduction
2. Market Overview
3. Our Solution
4. Features
5. Architecture
6. Results
7. Next Steps

Strong ghost deck:

1. Local component buying is fragmented, slow, and opaque.
2. Students waste trips because no one shows local price and stock together.
3. A basket-level comparison turns five shop visits into one plan.
4. The platform ranks shops by total cost, distance, and availability.
5. A pilot with 120 students cut sourcing time by 40%.
6. The same model works for any campus with a supplier network.
7. Fund the fall rollout to cover three colleges.

The second deck can be understood from the titles alone. That is the test.

## Length planning

Slide count follows time, not the amount of content you have.

| Talk length | Core slides | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 5 minutes | 5–7 | One idea, two or three evidence points, one ask |
| 10 minutes | 9–12 | Cut the literature review; get to the result |
| 15 minutes | 12–15 | One section per 3–4 minutes |
| 20 minutes | 15–20 | Leave 3–5 minutes for Q&A |
| 30 minutes | 20–28 | Use section dividers; plan one interaction |
| 45–60 minutes | 25–35 + appendix | Vary density; include a break or activity |
| Workshop (half day) | 20–30 + exercises | Slides support activities, not the reverse |

These are starting points. A visually driven keynote can use fewer slides; a technical review may need more. If you are over, cut a slide or an idea — do not shrink the type.

## Openings

Open with a reason to listen, not with a biography.

Strong openings:

- **A surprising fact** — "The average student visits four shops to buy one project."
- **A question** — "What would you do if the part you needed was 20 minutes away but 30% cheaper?"
- **A concrete story** — one user, one moment, one cost.
- **A before/after visual** — show the mess and the fix.
- **A bold claim** — "The problem is not supply. It is information."
- **A demo** — show the product working before explaining it.
- **A quote** — a customer or expert voice that sets the stakes.

Avoid:

- "Hi, I'm X, and today I'm going to talk about..."
- A long agenda slide before the audience knows why they should care.
- A company history that starts before the problem.
- Apologizing for the deck or the time.

The title slide can be simple: outcome-oriented title, subtitle with audience/date, no paragraph.

An agenda is a map, not a mandatory slide. Use it for a long or multi-part deck; skip it when the opening already gives the audience the structure. If you use one, make it short and outcome-oriented, not a table of contents.

## Transitions

Transitions are where most decks feel like a list instead of an argument. Write them explicitly in speaker notes.

Patterns:

- **Problem → solution:** "That is why the current process is expensive. Here is what changes."
- **Claim → evidence:** "That sounds plausible. The pilot data shows whether it holds."
- **Past → future:** "That got us here. The next constraint is different."
- **What → how:** "We know what needs to change. Now the mechanism."
- **Objection → answer:** "The obvious objection is cost. Here is the math."
- **Section change:** use a divider slide with a number and a short title, then one sentence of orientation.

Signpost sparingly. "There are three reasons" is useful; "as I mentioned earlier" is not.

## Closings

The close is the ask. Do not end on "Thank you" or a generic "Questions?"

A strong close:

1. **Recap the one idea** in a sentence.
2. **Paint the new bliss** — what the world looks like if they accept.
3. **Make a specific ask** — approve, fund, pilot, intro, sign, hire.
4. **Name the next step** — owner, date, and what happens after.

Example:

> "The pilot proved students will change where they buy when price and stock are visible. Approve the fall rollout for three colleges by June 1. We will report sourcing time and savings after eight weeks."

If the deck is a read-ahead, the final slide should still state the recommendation and decision requested.

## Deck types

### Pitch deck

**Goal:** earn a second meeting or an investment.

**Spine:**

1. Purpose — one declarative sentence.
2. Problem — who suffers, how much, and what they do today.
3. Solution — the eureka moment and why it is different.
4. Why now — the change that makes this possible.
5. Market — who pays, how many, and why the market is reachable.
6. Product — how it works, ideally with a demo or screenshot.
7. Traction — revenue, users, retention, pilots, logos.
8. Business model — pricing, unit economics, path to margin.
9. Competition — direct, indirect, and the status quo.
10. Team — why this team can win.
11. Financials — the few numbers that matter.
12. Ask — amount, use of funds, milestones.

**Length:** 10–12 core slides. The 10/20/30 rule (10 slides, 20 minutes, 30-point minimum) is a useful discipline for investor pitches, not a law for every deck.

**Must-have:** a one-sentence purpose, evidence of demand, a credible why-now, and a specific ask.

**Common mistakes:** feature list instead of value, no traction, vanity TAM, hiding the ask, too many slides, tiny fonts.

**Success test:** the investor asks "what is the next step?" rather than "what do you do?"

### Business or consulting review

**Goal:** make a decision.

**Spine:**

1. Answer — the recommendation in one page.
2. Three messages — the arguments that support it.
3. Evidence — one exhibit per message.
4. Risks — what could go wrong and how to mitigate it.
5. Ask — decision, owner, timing.

Use SCQA in the opening. Use MECE to structure the arguments. Use action titles on every slide.

**Length:** 8–12 slides plus appendix.

**Must-have:** an executive summary, action titles, one exhibit per slide, a clear recommendation.

**Common mistakes:** burying the answer, data dumps, no "so what", a 60-slide journey through the analysis.

**Success test:** a reader can read only the titles and make the decision.

### Academic or technical talk

**Goal:** communicate a contribution, defend it, or teach it.

**Spine:**

1. Title, authors, affiliations.
2. Motivation — the problem and why it matters.
3. Gap — what is missing in current work.
4. Question or hypothesis.
5. Approach — the idea in one diagram.
6. Method — enough detail to trust the result.
7. Results — 1–3 slides, one claim each.
8. Discussion — limitations, alternatives, threats to validity.
9. Conclusion — the contribution in one sentence.
10. Acknowledgments and questions.
11. Backup — detail for Q&A.

**Length:** about one slide per minute. A 10-minute talk is roughly 10–12 slides; a 20-minute talk is 15–20.

**Must-have:** a one-sentence contribution, a clear method diagram, results with axes, units, and sample sizes, and an honest limitations slide.

**Common mistakes:** a long literature review, too much method, unreadable figures, no clear takeaway, running out of time before results.

**Success test:** the audience can restate the contribution and why it matters.

### Conference keynote

**Goal:** change a belief or inspire action.

**Spine:**

1. Hook — a story, fact, or image that creates tension.
2. What is — the current reality and its cost.
3. What could be — the better future.
4. Three acts — evidence, examples, and contrast.
5. New bliss — make the future concrete.
6. Call to action — one thing the audience can do.

**Length:** 15–25 slides for an 18-minute talk, fewer if the visuals are strong.

**Must-have:** one big idea, stories, strong images, a memorable close.

**Common mistakes:** too many ideas, text-heavy slides, no emotional arc, no ask.

**Success test:** the audience repeats one sentence afterward.

### Training or workshop

**Goal:** learners can do something.

**Spine:**

1. Objectives — what learners will be able to do.
2. Why it matters — the consequence in their work.
3. Concept — the minimum theory.
4. Demo — show the task.
5. Practice — learners try it.
6. Feedback — common errors and corrections.
7. Recap — the checklist.
8. Job aid — what they take away.

**Length:** 15–30 slides for a one-hour session; chunk every 10–15 minutes with an activity.

**Must-have:** observable objectives, practice, feedback, recap, job aid.

**Common mistakes:** lecture dump, no practice, no recap, too much theory.

**Success test:** the learner performs the task without the slides.

### Sales deck

**Goal:** the buyer agrees to a next step.

**Spine:**

1. Insight — something the buyer does not know about their own problem.
2. Problem and status quo — the cost of doing nothing.
3. Solution — how it changes the outcome.
4. Proof — case study, data, demo, testimonial.
5. ROI — the business case in the buyer's numbers.
6. Implementation — effort, risk, timeline.
7. Next step — a specific meeting, pilot, or signature.

**Length:** 8–12 slides.

**Must-have:** buyer-specific insight, proof, ROI, clear next step.

**Common mistakes:** product features first, a generic deck, no next step, no business case.

**Success test:** the buyer commits to a pilot or a follow-up with the right people.

### Board or executive update

**Goal:** a decision or alignment.

**Spine:**

1. Decision requested — up front.
2. Three messages — what the board needs to know.
3. Evidence — financial and operational.
4. Risks — what could change the answer.
5. Recommendation and ask.

**Length:** 5–8 slides plus appendix.

**Must-have:** decision up front, evidence, risks, ask.

**Common mistakes:** too much detail, no decision, burying the ask on slide 14.

**Success test:** the decision is made in the meeting, not deferred for more analysis.

### Internal team update

**Goal:** alignment and action.

**Spine:**

1. Headline — what changed.
2. Progress against plan.
3. Metrics — the few that matter.
4. Risks and blockers.
5. Decisions and asks.
6. Next milestones.

**Length:** 5–8 slides.

**Must-have:** outcomes not activity, owners and dates, explicit asks.

**Common mistakes:** an activity report, no asks, too much detail.

**Success test:** the team knows what changed, what to do, and who owns it.

### Project demo

**Goal:** show that it works and get support.

**Spine:**

1. Hook — the problem in one story.
2. Demo — live or video, with a backup.
3. Architecture — one slide, only what the audience needs.
4. Results — what changed.
5. Differentiation — why this approach.
6. Roadmap — what happens next.
7. Ask.

**Length:** 8–12 slides.

**Must-have:** a working demo or video, backup screenshots, one architecture slide, results.

**Common mistakes:** demo without narrative, too much architecture, no backup, no ask.

**Success test:** the audience understands what it does, why it matters, and what to do next.

### Lightning talk or thesis defense

**Lightning talk (5 minutes):** 5–7 slides — hook, one idea, two or three evidence points, close. Cut everything else.

**Thesis defense (20 minutes):** 15–20 slides — contribution, motivation, gap, method, results, limitations, future work. Prepare backup slides for committee questions.

## Slide writing

### Action titles

An action title is a complete sentence that states the slide's conclusion. It is the most important text on the slide.

Rules:

1. Write a complete sentence.
2. Lead with the insight, not the data source.
3. Use active voice and a strong verb.
4. Quantify when you can.
5. Keep it under 15 words or two lines.
6. Make it specific enough to be challenged.
7. Match the body: the evidence must prove the title.

Examples:

| Weak topic title | Strong action title |
|---|---|
| Market Overview | The market is shifting from hardware to subscriptions |
| Customer Feedback | Customers rank setup speed above price |
| Q3 Revenue | Q3 revenue grew 14% on two enterprise contracts |
| Cost Reduction | Three initiatives will save $4.2M in year one |
| Architecture | One API layer keeps the AI, catalog, and shop data in sync |
| Next Steps | Approve the pilot by June 1 and we will report in eight weeks |

If a title cannot be disagreed with, it is decoration. If a title needs "and", it is two slides.

### Body copy

- One idea per line.
- Short fragments, not paragraphs.
- Three to five points maximum; fewer is better.
- Parallel structure across points.
- Concrete nouns and verbs; cut adjectives and filler.
- Active voice.
- Define jargon once, in plain language.
- Do not write what you will say aloud; write the guideposts.
- Use the slide for what the eye needs, the speaker notes for what the ear needs.

Weak: "Our platform provides a comprehensive suite of tools that enables users to optimize their purchasing decisions."

Strong: "Compare price, stock, and distance in one view."

### Cut the filler

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| in order to | to |
| utilize, leverage | use |
| at this point in time | now |
| a number of | several |
| has the ability to | can |
| due to the fact that | because |
| in the event that | if |
| prior to | before |
| subsequent to | after |
| in close proximity to | near |
| it is important to note that | (cut) |
| it should be noted that | (cut) |
| we would like to | we will |
| best-in-class, world-class | (replace with evidence) |
| innovative, cutting-edge | (show what is new) |
| comprehensive solution | (name what it does) |

If a word does not change the meaning, cut it. If a sentence does not change the decision, cut the sentence.

### Numbers

- Round to the precision that matters: 37%, not 37.2%.
- Compare: "40% faster than the current process", not "40% faster".
- Give context: previous period, target, benchmark, or cost of inaction.
- One hero number per stat slide.
- Label units, currency, sample size, and date.
- Put the source and date on the slide, not only in the appendix.
- If a number is surprising, explain why before the audience asks.

### Speaker notes

Speaker notes are cues, not a script. For each slide:

- the opening sentence;
- the evidence you will point to;
- the transition to the next slide;
- the time budget;
- the source, if it is not on the slide.

Write the first and last sentence verbatim if you tend to ramble. Keep the rest in fragments.

### Handouts and read-aheads

A live deck and a read-ahead are different products.

- A **live deck** supports a speaker. It can be sparse; the speaker supplies the argument.
- A **read-ahead** must stand alone. Add a one-page executive summary, the evidence the reader needs, and sources. Do not just shrink the live deck and email it.
- A **leave-behind** should include the ask, the next step, and contact details. It should make sense to someone who missed the meeting.
- If the same file must serve both, put the detail in the appendix and write speaker notes for the live version.

### Tone by audience

| Audience | Tone | What they want |
|---|---|---|
| Executives | Direct, answer-first | The decision, the evidence, the risk |
| Investors | Confident, concrete | Traction, why now, the ask |
| Technical peers | Precise, honest | Method, results, limitations |
| Students | Clear, relatable | Why it matters, how it works, what to do |
| Customers | Empathetic, outcome-led | Their problem, proof, next step |
| Public audience | Human, visual, story-led | One idea they can repeat |

## Narrative anti-patterns

- **Chronological dump** — "first we did this, then we did that." Reorganize around the argument.
- **Agenda-first** — the audience does not yet care about your sections.
- **"About us" first** — the audience cares about their problem before your history.
- **Feature list** — features are evidence, not the argument.
- **Burying the ask** — put the decision on the first or last slide, not slide 14.
- **Too many ideas** — if everything is important, nothing is.
- **No tension** — without a complication, there is no reason to act.
- **No evidence** — a claim without proof is an opinion.
- **No human element** — one user, one story, one quote makes the argument memorable.
- **Ending on "Thank you"** — the last slide should ask for something.
- **Reading the slides** — the audience can read faster than you can speak.
